Paper prototyping a themed tabletop game

TEAM

TIMELINE
September 2026

TOOLS
Figma, Microsoft Excel, Sketchpad + Pens



OVERVIEW
As part of a game design course, our team was tasked to make tabletop game with the theme "Repair" within an hour. Together, we took inspiration from games we knew, brainstormed over 10 game ideas and combined them into 3 bigger games. We decided on our game and used Figma to build our prototype as we finalized the game's rules. We challenged ourselves to come up with a tabletop that was fun, memorable, and replayable - we succeeded at this task with 'Monkey Business'.


ROLE
I penned all of our brainstorming ideas and combined them to draft three games. I also calculated our overall effort, excitement, and marketability to filter our final game and played an important role in drafting its rules.

THE PROTOTYPE

The game ‘Monkey Business’ is a tabletop game that consists of three players attempting to fix a model together. However, one of the players cannot hear, one cannot see, and one cannot talk.
  • The mute player has a deck of cards, each card consisting of various colored blocks and pieces that are oriented in different ways. After drawing one, the mute player attempts to explain the type of block and other properties of the model on the card to the player that cannot hear by using only their bodies.
  • The deaf player further tries to interpret the mute player’s signs to guide the blind player, who is responsible for assembling the model together from nothing by feeling each block.
  • As the hands of all three players are hidden behind boards, the mute player cannot see the assembly as the blind player fixes it.
  • Once the players think they have completed the model, they lift their boards to reveal how well they replicated the model on the card.
This can be a challenge for 3 players to figure out how to repair a model from the ground up, or a competition between 6 players to find the team that cooperates best in a 3v3.

THE CONTEXT

The theme of our project was “Repair”. As a group of three, we worked together to contribute ideas of games that we knew and mesh them together to match the theme.
  • The note-taker was responsible for penning the ideas down as they were shared. We took turns adding to each others' ideas and picked our game by combining ideas from the list.
  • The prototype designer made the prototype image as seen in Image 1. They created a rough draft of our game idea and added to it as we edited our rules.
  • We all acted as rule drafters and designed the items and rules that were part of the game.
One of our challenges was adding the repair element to the game. Although we picked the idea with the easiest implementation, it was still important that we figured out how to communicate to players that something was to be repaired with cooperation. We had a time limit of 20 minutes to make our game and had to make a tabletop with high replay value. Furthermore, it had to be made with a small budget and be simple enough for immediate implementation.


BRAINSTORMING

Our process included finding inspiration from pre-existing games, implementing a “repair” element, and modifying the rules to make it more interactive. Our game ideas mainly focused on board games, but we took inspiration from online games, as well, our most pivotal being 'Boombananas!'. We came up with 9 varying ideas that involved fixing something in different formats, such as sliding puzzles, cards, and collections. We found it easy to come up with ideas as the theme was broad and we had previous experience with fun board games, so we knew what to look for. We spent more time taking these ideas and compiling the most fun ones into bigger games with better objectives and rules.



Img 2. Brainstorming list of 9 ideas, later compiled into 3 bigger games. The shapes refer to ideas that we wanted to piece together.



CHOOSING AN IDEA

We created a filter list of the three games listed at the bottom of our brainstorm list, which were a compilation of some of our favorite ideas. The final games were:
  • Monkey Business: A tabletop where deaf, mute, and blind players work together to build something from a manual's instructions.
  • Spooky Scraps: A board game where players traverse a dark board to fix broken furniture while being chased by a monster (played by a player).
  • The Dog Did It!: A board game where a player is secretly a child who broke a random item. The other players must figure out who the child is, while searching the house via the board to find the broken item.
We rated the games together on a scale of 5 in categories that mattered the most to us - personal excitement, marketability, and effort in production. Although The Dog Did It! won, we felt as though it might be too similar to an existing board game, Clue, and we might have issues with implementing the “repair” element seamlessly in a board game. Spooky Scraps was placed last and was more inclined to becoming an online game. Monkey Business succeeded and was the easiest to implement within the time constraint.

PLAYTESTING

Part of our feedback involved how we would present different blocks and items to the blind player while making it complex enough to be a puzzle. We wanted to prioritize inclusivity with the theme.

We decided on using simple blocks with colors (as seen in Image 1) and potentially using clay to model some of the items. Furthermore, we were concerned that there was too much similarity to the game that inspired us - ‘Boombananas!’, and we decided to expand on the types of items from there into a variety that could be “felt”. We simplified the initial “manual” design into cards so that it could allow some creative freedom for the blind player to construct things with a playful surprise element in the reveal of the final outcome.


INSIGHTS & ITERATIONS

Other than building our prototype into a game and having more playtests with feedback, one element that we changed towards the end of our project cycle was the interactions of the deaf player -

The deaf player is reserved to being a translator for the mute and blind player. To fix this, we decided that the deaf player would share a board with the blind player, i.e, the deaf player could be shown a component by the blind player, who is fixing the puzzle together. This way, we can add colors to the game instead of just shapes - these colors can be identified by the deaf player. We can have two of the same item be colored differently so that the blind player depends upon the deaf player to tell the difference between the colors. This way, we gave the deaf player more to do than simply re-iterating what the mute player is trying to say to enhance their engagement. Furthermore, it adds the variety that we were missing before our playtest.

If provided more time and an increased budget, we would draft more items other than simple shapes. We would test the game and draft our rules to ensure all players are actively involved in the process of constructing their piece. Additionally, we would provide the deaf player with construction safety noise-cancelling headgear, the blind player with a blindfold, and the mute player with a hard hat to really develop the atmosphere and design elements of the game.
