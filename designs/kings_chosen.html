OVERVIEW
Inspired by Manfred Ludwig's 'Viva Topo!', our goal was to create a board game within three weeks utilizing elements of its original game. We used Google Slides to create our game board and playtested using this tool, as well. Since we were limited to a digital space, I utilized Procreate to design assets and Google Docs to write the rulebook.

The outcome was The King's Chosen, a medieval-fantasy themed game, where players play as army generals and work to keep their armies alive and successful. Their armies fight a dragon while assisting villagers around the kingdom to earn Prestige points and show why they belong in the King's Chosen.


ROLE
As the game designer, I had full control over the design, rules, and playtesting of the game. I designed assets, wrote and compiled the rules in a rulebook, conducted playtesting sessions, and iterated the design based on the playtest feedback.

DESIGN PROCESS

  • Interpreting the prompt:
     I utilized theme-driven design before implementing mechanic-driven design, as I had already set my mind on creating a medieval-fantasy themed game. I imagined the 'Viva Topo!' world and characters first within the theme's setting, then developed the mechanics to support the theme. I wanted to ensure that players understood the rules and mechanics despite the aesthetic, so I tested my rules on the initial Viva Topo! board before creating The King's Chosen board.


  • Inspirations:
    I enjoy playing fantastical adventure games like Skyrim and Baldur's Gate 3. Having much to do and mythical elements such as enchanted weapons and otherworldly creatures piques my interest often, so it felt the most exciting to pursue. Moreover, when I played "Viva Topo!", I felt as though there should be a way to attack the pursuer and use the environment to my advantage, so the idea of slaying the dragon and helping villagers for advantages fell into place naturally. Conducting playtests with players that don't often play medieval-fantasy games and watching them enjoy it only made me more enthusiastic about the idea.


  • Design Challenges:
    One of the biggest challenges observed during playtest 1 was figuring out how to include an environmental aspect (i.e, surrounding villages) without making the game complicated or overwhelmingly long. During my first rules draft, players enjoyed the idea of slaying the dragon so much that they did not utilize the villages for shelter. Therefore, I introduced the concept of "village quests" (side objectives to earn points and advantages against the dragon) to urge players to visit the villages in the board corners. This worked very well, as observed in my second playtest!
    Other challenges related to edge-cases in the rulebook, which were eventually clarified further.

PLAYTESTING

I conducted playtesting sessions with various participants to observe how they interpreted the rules, how they interacted with the game, the emotions they felt, and the challenges or successes they encountered. Each play session involved either two completely new players or one new player and myself, the developer.

What I Learned:
  • Players will encounter what a developer considers "edge cases" more often than expected. Playtesting allows us to find those unexpected scenarios and therefore better refine the game rules.

  • The roll of the dice is a key element in maintaining tension and excitement, because randomness is always new and unexpected. Anything can happen at any time. This is why they will pursue the events that make them feel this excitement.

  • Players like having autonomy and therefore multiple ways to approach the same goal.

  • Players like having a close reference of things so that they don't have to keep track of everything by themselves. This could be a visual guide in the rule book, trackers on the game board, etc.


What Was Changed:
  • Core functioning of village tiles now used "village quests" and "village perks", using a new die called the "Village Die". Players could roll a die to help villagers and gain perks depending on the village they assist, giving them the upper hand against the dragon. They also gained more life points against the dragon while fighting in a village. What were previously "safe zones" as seen in the "Viva Topo!" was changed into a place of great advantage, if strategized around it them appropriately.

  • End-Game Rewards added. I wanted players to have other ways to win instead of slaying the dragon, as previously, killing the dragon automatically gave you a game-winning number of points. With game rewards, players who complete moving around the board, players who attack the dragon the most, and players who help the most villages get enough bonus points to change the game's outcome.

  • Introuced trackers to main game board. Most participants seemed to struggle keeping track of their progress, so the empty space on the Google slide was used to display trackers that monitered their progress towards various goals.

  • Dragon health is now based on the number of players. Having a fixed health made the game feel longer than it needed to be. Therefore, each player adds +25 to the dragon's health.


INSIGHTS & ITERATIONS

Successes:
  • Watching my friends playtest the game during playtest 2 and play for over 1.5 hours - knowing they enjoyed it so much made me feel very accomplished and satisfied with my end product.

  • Making each village have a unique perk and the concept of village quests was a successful way to encourage players to utilize the villages more.

  • Adding reward points at the end of the game helped players feel like slaying the dragon wasn't the only way to win, providing more room for various strategies.

  • Players only had to read through the rules once to understand the game and enjoy it to the fullest.

  • Dice roll mechanics made players feel like the game was "intense" and "engaging". The randomness of the dice rolls added much excitement to the gameplay. This was increased by adding a separate, village die as well.

  • Players found the trackers intuitive to use.

  • Players were comfortable stating how and where the would modify the game to improve it, also adding if a rule was a game flaw versus bad luck.


Future Changes:

The most prominent comment during my second iteration was that the game could be shorter, considering the multiple things that were being tracked. If another iteration was possible, I would like to try the game with real dice and attempt an offline format of board game to reduce the complexity of using an online interface and therefore the time required for gameplay. With regards to mechanics, I would polish some rules related to the villages - players wanted to be able to roll for a village quest immediately after reaching a village (previously, they had to wait until their next turn, which proved to be tedious). Furthermore, if players strategize around attacking the dragon at a village for more health, they should be able to do so twice - one for each dirt path the dragon crosses. Some suggested reducing the number of perks that a player can have at once. As the villages were a new addition to the game around the second iteration, I would like to go back and refine them in the future.
